# Home blood pressure: categories, thresholds and how to measure

*General knowledge, not findings about anyone. Guidelines change; check the date above.
Not medical advice.*

## 1. The same number can get different labels

Guideline bodies use different categories, and some use different thresholds at home
than in the office.

| Guideline | Category | Office | Home average |
|---|---|---|---|
| AHA/ACC | Normal | <120 and <80 | |
| AHA/ACC | Elevated | 120 to 129 and <80 | |
| AHA/ACC | Stage 1 hypertension | 130 to 139 or 80 to 89 | 130/80, same as office |
| AHA/ACC | Stage 2 hypertension | ≥140 or ≥90 | 135/85 |
| ESC 2024 | Elevated BP | 120/70 to <140/90 | 120/70 to <135/85 |
| ESC 2024 | Hypertension | ≥140/90 | ≥135/85 |
| ESH 2021 (home monitoring) | Hypertension | ≥140/90 | ≥135/85 |

All values in mmHg. Empty cells: no home value given in the sources used here.

The AHA/ACC office categories are unchanged from 2017 in the 2025 guideline, which
recommends home monitoring to confirm an office diagnosis [1]. The ESH 2021 position
paper describes the ACC/AHA guideline as setting the home level for stage 1 at the same
130/80 as in the office, and treating 135/85 at home as equivalent to stage 2; the paper
itself keeps 135/85 as the home threshold and notes that the lower ACC/AHA values are
mostly based on observational surveys [2]. ESC 2024 kept hypertension at office ≥140/90
and added *elevated BP* [3, 4]; the home column comes from a table in a commentary by
the Spanish Society of Cardiology's guideline working group [5]. ESC 2024 recommends out
of office measurement for diagnosis, because it detects white coat and masked
hypertension [6], and emphasizes that blood pressure lowering helps people with high
cardiovascular risk even when blood pressure is only elevated [7]. What an elevated
average means therefore depends on overall risk.

## 2. How home blood pressure is measured for a diagnosis

From the European Society of Hypertension's 2021 position paper on home monitoring [2]:

| Element | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Device | Clinically validated upper arm cuff; wrist and finger devices not generally recommended |
| Before measuring | 5 minutes seated rest |
| Readings | Two per occasion, 1 minute apart |
| Schedule | Morning and evening, for 7 days (not fewer than 3) |
| Threshold | Average ≥135/85 mmHg indicates hypertension |
| First day | The paper calls discarding it debated |

A pooled analysis of 2,122 people found first day readings about **2.8/1.4 mmHg
higher** than the following days, with values settling after day 3. Its authors
recommend 7 days with a minimum of 3, and discarding day 1, particularly in the 3 day
schedule [8].

A validated cuff is not error free. The ISO 81060-2 validation standard accepts a mean
difference from the reference of up to 5 mmHg, with a standard deviation up to 8 mmHg
[9].

**Single readings taken at random moments are not what these thresholds were built
for.** The position paper's conditions (seated rest first, two readings, a fixed
schedule) exist because circumstances change a reading [2].

## 3. When a single reading is an emergency

The American Heart Association describes readings **higher than 180/120 mmHg** as severe
hypertension: wait at least a minute and measure again. If it stays that high, it needs
attention from a health care professional right away; with symptoms such as chest pain,
shortness of breath, weakness, or changes in vision or speech, call emergency services
[10].
